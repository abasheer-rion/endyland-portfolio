Argus One

The vision engine behind Argus. It reads store camera frames on a cloud GPU, tracks each person, estimates pose and builds a score over time that decides when to raise an alert.

Engine, streaming agent and deployment, March to October 2026. I handed this work off when I left in 2026. Python, PyTorch, Ultralytics YOLOv8, FastAPI and OpenCV.

Per-frame models

Frames arrive at about 3 per second per camera at 640 by 360. The engine runs inference on every second or third frame and redraws the last result on the others for the live view. Each inferred frame goes through these stages.

StageModelSettings and output
DetectionYOLOv8 from Ultralytics, COCO-pretrained, small size on the live enginesWhole frame at confidence 0.25, input 640. Classes used: person, backpack and handbag. Frames from different cameras are batched.
TrackingIn-house greedy IoU tracker, one per cameraRe-associates a track within a 12-second window. It is not ByteTrack or DeepSORT.
PoseYOLOv8n-poseRun once per person crop, minimum crop 32 px. Output is 17 COCO keypoints.
Bag typeOpenCLIP ViT-B/32, zero-shotRuns only when a bag is detected. Separates a store basket or bag from a personal bag so that bag interaction is not falsely flagged. Threshold 0.55.
Concealment checkMoondream2, a small vision-language model in fp16One fixed yes or no question on selected frames. Its answer can veto an alert. A read takes 300 to 650 ms.

The detector and pose weights are the COCO-pretrained Ultralytics releases. They were not fine-tuned on store footage.

Once per alert

After an alert latches, a heavier pass runs on the trigger frame: YOLO detection at confidence 0.5, pose on padded crops, a YOLOv8 classifier trained on the public UCF-Crime dataset for shoplifting versus normal, and EVA02-CLIP-L/14 with a 224 by 224 input and a 1024-number embedding.

Re-identification

Re-identification joins one person across cameras and short gaps. Argus serves an in-house ResNet-18 encoder with 512-number embeddings. In October 2026 a bake-off compared it with four open encoders on 9,216 labelled pairs, 7,129 of the same person and 2,087 of different people, from 1,377 clips and 22 cameras.

From detections to an alert

Pose gives 22 features per person, including hand-to-torso distance, elbow angle, torso angle, body height ratio and hand speed over a 10-frame window.

Two scores are computed. The frame score runs from 0 to 100 and is a weighted sum of seven signals: hand near pockets or waistband, pose, time in one spot, concealment, bag interaction, object change and hand movement. It is shown on the operator tile and triggers nothing.

The evidence score is kept per tracked person. It adds up behaviour over seconds, decays with a 25-second half-life and follows an ordered sequence of scan, reach, conceal and depart. The alert latches at 80 and needs a chained concealment gesture. This is the only trigger for an incident, and the Moondream2 check can veto the crossing.

Clip and webhook

On a latch the engine takes 15 seconds before and 1.5 seconds after the trigger from an in-memory ring buffer of the original JPEG frames, encodes an MP4, uploads it to Cloudflare R2 and posts an HMAC-SHA256-signed webhook to the platform API, with 3 retries. The pre-roll is 15 seconds so a reviewer can judge the clip.

Inference runtime

PyTorch and Ultralytics run in-process in the FastAPI server in half precision. TensorRT and ONNX are not used for the main models. A rented RTX 4090 on RunPod served until September 2026, then AWS GPU instances. On 17 August 2026 one 4090 held 20 of its 24 camera slots.

Batching detection

Frames from different cameras are grouped into a batch of up to 8 with a 10 ms wait. Over 338 frames before and 338 after, average inference time fell from 74.5 ms to 46.3 ms, a factor of 1.61, and dropped frames fell from 5.5% to 1.0%.

Pose is not batched

Batching person crops of different sizes shifted keypoints by up to 43 px and changed the person count in 2 of 18 test cases, so pose stays at one crop at a time, about 7.9 ms each. Whole-frame pose was faster, 8.23 ms for one frame and 2.32 ms per frame at batch 16, but it missed 2 of 18 people, and keypoints decide alerts, so it was not adopted.

The limit was Python

Under load the engine dropped about 2.5 frames per second while the GPU sat at 9% compute. Sixteen inference threads shared one Python interpreter lock, and the resident models held 24.0 of 24.6 GB of VRAM. The fix was separate inference processes. The live configuration runs 2 processes with 8 workers.

Labels and training data

Labels come from four places, and every label records which model or person produced it.

  • Manual tool. A Streamlit app from November 2025 recorded theft yes or no, a risk score, eight behaviour flags and timeline markers.
  • Auto-labeller. From July 2026 a script pulls a stored incident clip, extracts 4 to 8 evenly spaced frames at most 768 px wide and asks a hosted vision-language model for a structured verdict: theft, benign or inconclusive, with a confidence, a one-line reason and a few visual attributes. The prompt withholds the system’s own risk score so the label stays independent.
  • Human grading. From 7 August 2026 operators graded incidents in the ops console: 164 verdicts from 3 graders in the first two days.
  • Activity labels. 552 clips were sorted into 11 activity classes, such as staff behind the counter (139 clips) and paying at the till (126), by one hosted model. Its agreement with three other models ranged from 0.32 to 0.70.

The labels sit in an append-only Postgres table, so agreement between labellers is a single GROUP BY query. On 8 August 2026 it held 905 labels. Every one was a false positive or uncertain, and none was a confirmed theft.

The first run exposed a leak. Agreement with the teacher was 0.76 on clips recorded with the on-screen overlay and 0.36 on clean clips, so the student was reading the system’s burned-in captions. After that, training and gating used clean clips only.

The second run’s 0.9925 agreement does not show skill on theft. The eval set is 99.85% benign, so always answering benign scores 0.9985, which is higher. The tuned model never output theft across 920 clips, and its recall on the single theft clip in the eval set was 0. The corpus had 17 theft-labelled clips in training and 1 in the clean eval set, out of 6,987 manifest rows.

The fine-tune taught the model the output format and matched the teacher on benign clips. It could not be validated on theft because the stores had almost no confirmed theft clips.

Around the loop: five gates fixed before training, two of which refuse to pass on thin evidence; a committed eval-split lock holding 698 ids and hashes but no pixels; a rule and a test that the student never trains on its own labels; nightly labelling and retrain workflows in GitHub Actions; and a ledger of model generations.

What was not measured

The repo records no end-to-end alert latency and no precision or recall for the detector on store footage. Recall could not be measured because the label ledger held no confirmed thefts.
